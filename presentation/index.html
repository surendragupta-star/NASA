Hi, we are team SURENDRA. This is Crew Health Console, our answer to the challenge “Create Health Monitoring Software for Astronauts on Space Missions.” It helps astronauts check their own health during long missions, understand what the results mean in space, and decide whether to handle a problem themselves or call a flight surgeon.

Crew Health Console · NASA’s five hazards of human spaceflight

NASA’s Human Research Program describes five hazards of spaceflight: radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, altered gravity, and a hostile closed environment. Together they cause bone loss, heart and blood-pressure changes, vision problems, a weaker immune system, poor sleep and low mood. The farther a crew goes, the longer help takes. On Mars, a reply from a flight surgeon can take up to 48 minutes, so astronauts must be able to check themselves and act.

Crew Health Console · How it works

Crew Health Console answers three questions. How am I doing? Astronauts log 11 indicators that standard onboard equipment can measure, from heart rate and blood pressure to sleep, mood, radiation dose and body mass, plus any symptoms. Why does it matter up here? Every field and body system has plain-language explanations. Should I act or call the ground? Each reading is graded Nominal, Watch or Act now, and every flag comes with a specific next step.

Crew Health Console · Environment settings

The console knows where the crew is. Pick a space station, deep-space transit, a lunar base, a rover or a Mars base, and it adjusts the expected radiation dose, gravity, bone-loss estimate and signal delay. The escalation advice changes too. Near Earth, the crew sends the report and follows the ground. On Mars, where replies take up to 48 minutes, the console tells the crew to start the onboard medical checklist right away. Radiation rates come from NASA research and from measurements by the Curiosity rover and the Chang’e 4 lander.

Crew Health Console · Gamification and reporting

Health only helps if it becomes a habit. A timed daily checklist covers vitals, glucose, mood, two hours of exercise, fluids, a dosimeter reading, a private call home and a weekly eye check. Tasks tick off automatically as data comes in, earning points, levels, streaks and badges. When something needs the ground, one click produces a structured report of the day’s vitals, symptoms and flags for the flight surgeon.

Impact & next steps

Impact

Early warning on slow declines. Clear decisions on when to call the ground. Also useful at Antarctic stations, in submarines and on analog missions.
